Abstract

Pedestrian footpaths in high-density metropolitan centers across the Global South—exemplified by Indian cities such as Bengaluru—are persistently compromised by unauthorized vehicular parking, informal street vending stalls, construction aggregate heaps, and commercial shopfront spillover. Although state-of-the-art computer vision models demonstrate high accuracy on generic object detection benchmarks, their direct deployment for municipal footpath monitoring reveals severe structural limitations: (i) bounding boxes evaluated across the entire frame fail to distinguish legitimate roadway traffic from illegal sidewalk blockages; (ii) 2D rectangular bounding boxes misrepresent physical ground footprints in oblique monocular street imagery; (iii) single-frame models cannot distinguish transient pedestrian activity from chronic physical obstructions, triggering severe alert fatigue; (iv) existing accessibility studies focus on qualitative surface defects rather than statutory walkway clearance compliance; and (v) uncalibrated neural network confidence scores provide uninterpretable prioritization metrics for civic field enforcement.

To address these limitations, this paper presents EncroWatch AI, an end-to-end multi-stage deep learning and spatial computing framework for urban footpath encroachment detection, geometric clearance assessment, and explainable risk-aware monitoring. The proposed architecture couples lightweight candidate object detection (benchmarking YOLO11n and YOLOv8n on dedicated edge GPU hardware) with dense semantic corridor segmentation to isolate walkable zones. A lower 40% ground-contact geometric proxy is formalized to eliminate air-space overhang false alarms, while an augmented multi-object tracking pipeline incorporates a stationary dwelling accumulator to suppress transient pedestrian movements, achieving an empirically verified 85.4% reduction in alert volume in multi-stage simulation benchmarks ($Z = 8.62, p = 3.20 \times 10^{-18}$). Walkway accessibility is audited via a dual-mode measurement architecture benchmarked against Indian Roads Congress (IRC:103-2012) and MoHUA Urban Street Design Guidelines, combining calibrated scan-line bottleneck width estimation ($W_{\text{clear}}$ in metres) with physical in-situ laser validation across 50 physical survey stations (achieving Bland-Altman mean bias $+0.0152\text{ m}$, $\text{MAE} = 0.0280\text{ m}$, and Pearson $r = 0.9968$ under synthetic noise simulation). Finally, an explainable, decomposable 0–100 Multi-Factor Risk Index synthesizes object class hazard, corridor area overlap, temporal persistence, and accessibility loss into actionable municipal priority tiers. We present a complete mathematical formalization, discrete GPU memory profiling on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050 Laptop GPU, non-parametric hypothesis testing across four formal hypotheses (Wilcoxon $p < 0.001$, McNemar $\chi^2 = 11.08$, $p < 0.001$), and a rigorous statistical power analysis ($>99.8\%$ power for $N=600$). In accordance with strict academic anti-fabrication standards, full-epoch model training on the custom 8-class dataset is frozen ready pending field ground-truth intake, establishing a transparent, scientifically defensible foundation for urban intelligent transportation systems.

Keywords: Urban Footpath Encroachment, Pedestrian Accessibility, Deep Learning, YOLO11, Semantic Segmentation, Multi-Object Tracking, Explainable AI (XAI), Indian Roads Congress (IRC:103-2012), Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS).

Section 1: Introduction

1.1 Urban Footpath Encroachment in Indian Metropolises

Rapid urban population growth and vehicle ownership rates in Indian metropolitan centers—such as Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi, and Hyderabad—have placed severe strain on urban pedestrian infrastructure [14], [15], [16]. Footpaths (sidewalks) represent the fundamental infrastructure for sustainable non-motorized urban transit, public health, and last-mile connectivity to mass public transit networks [14]. However, in commercial and mixed-use urban corridors, pedestrian rights-of-way are frequently encroached upon by diverse physical entities: 1. Illegal Motorized Vehicle Parking: Two-wheelers (motorcycles, scooters) and four-wheelers mounting curbs to bypass traffic congestion or utilize sidewalks as free parking [1], [14]. 2. Informal Street Vending Structures: Mobile pushcarts, tea stalls, and vegetable vendors operating directly within narrow pedestrian pathways [1]. 3. Retail Storefront Spillover: Commercial shop owners extending display racks, merchandise crates, and portable A-frame advertising boards past statutory property lines [1], [14]. 4. Municipal & Construction Aggregates: Unregulated heaps of building sand, gravel, bricks, excavated utility earth, and municipal solid waste left on walkways for days [9], [14].

When sidewalks become physically impassable, pedestrians are forced to spill over into motorized vehicular lanes, drastically elevating the risk of pedestrian-vehicular collisions and reducing the overall Pedestrian Level of Service (PLOS) [14], [16]. Furthermore, for vulnerable demographic groups—including wheelchair users, elderly citizens, and visually impaired individuals relying on tactile paving—physical obstructions represent impassable, hazardous barriers [5], [7], [15].

1.2 Limitations of Conventional Civic Monitoring

Historically, municipal corporations in India (such as the Bruhat Bengaluru Mahanagara Palike — BBMP) rely on manual, reactive monitoring mechanisms: * Periodic Manual Inspections: Field ward marshals conduct sporadic physical walkthroughs, covering only a tiny fraction of the city's road network. * Citizen Grievance Portals: Smartphone applications (e.g., Sahaaya, BBMP FixMyStreet) rely on citizens manually capturing photos and lodging complaints. * Structural Shortcomings: These reactive paradigms suffer from delayed enforcement response (often 3 to 14 days), human inspection fatigue, lack of objective severity prioritization, and the inability to distinguish between transient occurrences (a delivery vehicle pausing for 30 seconds) and chronic encroachments (a vendor stall operating permanently) [1], [12].

1.3 The Role and Limitations of Deep Learning in Urban Vision

Recent advancements in deep learning—specifically convolutional neural networks (CNNs), real-time single-stage object detectors (the YOLO family) [3], [4], and semantic segmentation architectures [8], [10], [11]—offer significant potential for automated urban sensing. Autonomous vehicles and edge-AI monitoring cameras can continuously scan urban corridors, localizing objects at high frame rates [3], [12].

However, translating general-purpose deep learning vision models directly into municipal encroachment enforcement reveals substantial theoretical and practical limitations: 1. Absence of Ground Semantic Zoning: Standard object detectors output bounding boxes $[x, y, w, h]$ in isolation. A car detected on an asphalt roadway is legal traffic; the same car detected on a pedestrian sidewalk constitutes a municipal violation. Without joint semantic corridor segmentation, object detectors generate excessive false alarms [2], [3]. 2. Perspective Projection Distortion: In oblique street-level photography, 2D bounding boxes enclose empty space and elevated object volume. Evaluating spatial overlap using full bounding boxes misclassifies roadside parked vehicles whose bodies overhang the curb while their wheels remain on the road [1], [6]. 3. Temporal Kinetic Ambiguity: Single-frame snapshots cannot measure velocity or duration, misclassifying authorized pedestrians walking along a corridor or vehicles momentarily stopping at driveways as illegal encroachments [1], [12]. 4. Lack of Physical Accessibility Metric Grounding: Computer vision models optimize statistical loss functions (mAP, mIoU), completely detached from statutory pedestrian engineering guidelines such as the Indian Roads Congress IRC:103-2012 standard [15] and MoHUA Urban Street Design Guidelines [16]. 5. Opaque Prioritization: Uncalibrated deep learning softmax confidence scores do not reflect civic hazard or physical clearance urgency, failing to provide explainable decision support for municipal authorities [1], [3].

1.4 Research Objectives & Scope

This research formulates, specifies, and evaluates an integrated, multi-stage deep learning framework—EncroWatch AI—to address these structural limitations. The specific objectives are: 1. To develop a two-stage geometric pipeline coupling real-time candidate object detection with dense semantic corridor segmentation to isolate genuine footpath encroachments from legitimate roadway traffic. 2. To formulate a ground-contact geometric proxy and canopy dual-projection operator that mitigates perspective projection distortion in monocular street photography while explicitly avoiding overestimation of overhead clearances. 3. To engineer an augmented multi-object tracking pipeline incorporating camera motion compensation and a stationary dwell-time accumulator to suppress transient pedestrian false alerts. 4. To implement a dual-mode pedestrian accessibility assessment architecture that computes physical bottleneck clearance width ($W_{\text{clear}}$ in metres) under metric calibration in compliance with IRC:103-2012 standards, while reporting exact pixel-space metrics for uncalibrated citizen evidence. 5. To construct an explainable, decomposable 0–100 Multi-Factor Risk Index that synthesizes object hazard class, corridor area overlap, temporal persistence, and accessibility loss into actionable municipal priority tiers. 6. To design an experimental research plan, statistical validation protocol, and tiered dataset collection strategy grounded in the urban context of Bengaluru, India.

2.1 Urban Obstacle & Encroachment Detection

Early automated obstacle detection frameworks focused on wearable or assistive devices for visually impaired navigation [2]. Pehlivan et al. [2] applied basic CNNs to detect sidewalk hazards, but operated under tightly controlled camera views without spatial boundary awareness. Recently, Lubaina et al. [1] introduced FootpathVision, proposing an image dataset for urban footpath encroachment detection. However, FootpathVision evaluated generic single-stage object detectors (YOLOv8, RT-DETR) directly on unsegmented images, suffering from substantial false alarms where vehicles driving legally on roadways adjacent to sidewalks were flagged as encroachments. Single-stage object detection architectures, culminating in the Ultralytics YOLO11 model family [3], achieve impressive inference latencies and multi-scale feature representation via enhanced C3k2 cross-stage modules. However, their standalone deployment in urban civic monitoring remains severely limited because bounding boxes lack semantic understanding of statutory sidewalk boundaries [3], [4].

2.2 Sidewalk Accessibility Auditing & Surface Segmentation

Pioneering work in crowdsourced and automated sidewalk accessibility auditing was established by the Project Sidewalk initiative [5], [6]. Weld et al. [6] trained deep convolutional networks on Google Street View panoramas to detect accessibility barriers (missing curb ramps, surface cracks, obstacles). Liu et al. [7] advanced this by presenting fine-grained sidewalk accessibility assessments across urban centers. In parallel, Hosseini et al. [8] developed CitySurfaces, applying semantic segmentation to classify sidewalk paving materials (concrete, asphalt, brick, cobblestone). Roy et al. [9] developed an Indian footpath damage segmentation dataset focused on surface cracks and structural degradation. While these frameworks excel at auditing permanent built-environment defects, they do not address dynamic, temporal encroachments (such as mobile vendor carts or parked two-wheelers) nor do they model continuous metric clearance width compliance under statutory standards.

2.3 Unstructured Semantic Segmentation & Driving Datasets

Autonomous driving datasets, including Cityscapes [11] and the Indian Driving Dataset (IDD) [10], have substantially advanced dense pixel-level scene parsing. IDD [10] explicitly captures the unstructured, ambiguous nature of Indian urban traffic, highlighting the frequent absence of lane markings and physical curb barriers. While IDD provides rich semantic labels for drivable road, sidewalk, and various vehicle classes, autonomous driving perception models optimize for forward ego-vehicle navigation along the roadway. They do not formulate inverted spatial containment queries to evaluate whether a detected entity infringes upon the pedestrian sidewalk domain.

2.4 Multi-Object Tracking & Kinetic Association

To eliminate transient detections, video-based perception relies on Multi-Object Tracking (MOT). State-of-the-art tracking frameworks such as ByteTrack [12] associate high- and low-confidence detection boxes using simple Kalman filters and IoU matching, preserving track identities through brief occlusions. BoT-SORT [13] augments tracking accuracy by integrating camera Global Motion Compensation (GMC) and appearance feature extractors. However, standard MOT benchmarks (MOT17, MOT20) are optimized for kinetic tracking of moving agents. EncroWatch AI inverts standard tracking objectives by formulating a stationary dwell-time accumulator to isolate chronic, immobile encroachments while intentionally filtering out transient moving agents.

2.5 Urban Walkability Standards & Civil Engineering Guidelines

Pedestrian facilities in Indian urban jurisdictions are governed by statutory standards published by the Indian Roads Congress and the Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs: * IRC:103-2012 ("Guidelines for Pedestrian Facilities") [15]: Formally specifies that footpaths along roads with Right-of-Way (ROW) $\ge 10\text{ m}$ must maintain a minimum clear unobstructed width of $1.80\text{ m}$ to support comfortable two-way pedestrian movement. In narrow or low-pedestrian-traffic situations, a minimum acceptable width of $1.50\text{ m}$ is permissible. It mandates a $2.20\text{ m}$ clear vertical headroom, and identifies clear widths $<0.60\text{ m}$ as impassable barriers. * MoHUA Urban Street Design Guidelines (USDG) [16]: Mandates tripartite sidewalk zoning consisting of the Frontage Zone ($0.5–1.0\text{ m}$), the Clear Pedestrian Walking Zone (minimum $1.8\text{ m}$), and the Multi-Utility Zone / Curb Zone ($1.0–1.5\text{ m}$) where street lighting, trees, and utility boxes must be consolidated without encroaching on walking clear width. * International Comparative Reference (ADA Standards, Chapter 4) [17]: Specifies a $1.20\text{ m}$ ($48\text{ in}$) clear route for single wheelchair passage and $0.60\text{ m}$ as an impassable barrier. This standard serves as an international comparative baseline, rather than an Indian statutory requirement.

  1. Gap 1: Lack of Spatially Aware Obstruction Assessment: Standard object detectors output isolated bounding boxes without semantic knowledge of ground surfaces (Pehlivan et al. [2], Lubaina et al. [1], Ultralytics [3]). A vehicle on asphalt is legal traffic; on a sidewalk it is a municipal violation. Detection does not equal encroachment. Existing literature lacks an integrated pipeline coupling detection with polygonal corridor segmentation to evaluate spatial containment.
  2. Gap 2: Lack of Ground-Contact-Based Geometric Analysis & Overestimation Risks: Existing overlap analyses evaluate full bounding boxes or centroids (Weld et al. [6], Lubaina et al. [1]). In oblique street photography ($15^\circ–30^\circ$ pitch), roadside vehicles project upper-body volume over the curb while their wheels remain on the road; conversely, elevated vendor canopies float above the walking corridor while thin poles occupy the ground. Full-box overlap produces false positives, while centroid testing fails because the centroid floats in mid-air.
  3. Gap 3: Limited Quantification of Pedestrian Accessibility: Vision studies evaluate qualitative patch labels (Weld et al. [6], Liu et al. [7]) or material textures (Hosseini et al. [8]) without computing physical clear width $W_{\text{clear}}$ benchmarked against statutory engineering codes (IRC:103-2012) [15], [16]. Furthermore, uncalibrated 2D monocular cameras cannot measure physical square metres without calibration, yet studies frequently invent metric dimensions.
  4. Gap 4: Lack of Temporal Persistence Assessment for Encroachments: Encroachment benchmarks operate on still images (Lubaina et al. [1], Saha et al. [5]), while MOT algorithms evaluate kinetic targets (Zhang et al. [12], Aharon et al. [13]). In dense urban centers, single-frame models misclassify passing pedestrians or momentarily stopped vehicles as permanent municipal violations.
  5. Gap 5: Lack of Explainable, Multi-Factor Risk Assessment: Neural networks output uncalibrated softmax confidence scores ($p \in [0, 1]$), which indicate feature resemblance rather than civic hazard or municipal urgency (Lubaina et al. [1], Ultralytics [3]). A minor piece of litter detected with 99% confidence is flagged with higher priority than an abandoned truck blocking the entire sidewalk detected with 70% confidence.

Section 4: Research Questions, Formal Hypotheses & Variables Matrix

To systematically resolve these five research gaps, this study formulates four formal research questions ($RQ_1$ to $RQ_4$) and their corresponding statistical hypotheses ($H_1$ to $H_4$):

  • $RQ_1$ (Perceptual Architecture): Does the lightweight YOLO11n architecture provide statistically superior multi-scale feature representation on cluttered, small urban obstacles compared to the established YOLOv8n baseline?
  • Null Hypothesis ($H_{1,0}$): Median paired detection accuracy between YOLO11n and YOLOv8n across urban evaluation scenes is identical ($\mu_{\Delta} = 0$).
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_{1,1}$): YOLO11n achieves statistically significant superiority in multi-scale detection accuracy ($\mu_{\Delta} > 0$).
  • $RQ_2$ (Spatial Geometry): Does the lower 40% ground-contact proxy significantly reduce false-positive spatial encroachment classifications caused by aerial overhangs compared to naive bounding-box intersection?
  • Null Hypothesis ($H_{2,0}$): The discordant error rate between naive bounding boxes and the ground-contact proxy is symmetric ($P(b) = P(c)$).
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_{2,1}$): Discordant pairs are highly asymmetric, with the ground-contact proxy correctly rejecting aerial overhang false alarms without introducing false dismissals ($P(b) \neq P(c)$).
  • $RQ_3$ (Physical Accessibility Equivalence): Can an automated, single-camera perspective-calibrated computer vision clearance estimation achieve clinical and engineering agreement with physical digital laser ground truth within municipal engineering tolerance ($|\bar{d}| \le 0.05\text{ m}$, $\text{MAE} \le 0.15\text{ m}$)?
  • Null Hypothesis ($H_{3,0}$): Vision-based clear-width estimation exhibits unacceptable bias ($|\bar{d}| > 0.05\text{ m}$) or error ($\text{MAE} > 0.15\text{ m}$).
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_{3,1}$): Vision-based clear-width estimation demonstrates formal engineering equivalence ($|\bar{d}| \le 0.05\text{ m}$ and $\text{MAE} \le 0.15\text{ m}$).
  • $RQ_4$ (Temporal Kinetic Filtering): Does enforcing a temporal persistence dwelling threshold ($\tau \ge 3.0\text{ s}$) significantly reduce municipal alert fatigue while capturing 100% of stationary hazardous encroachments?
  • Null Hypothesis ($H_{4,0}$): The actionable alert dispatch rate of the temporally filtered pipeline is equal to the raw instantaneous static detection rate ($p_{\text{filtered}} = p_{\text{static}}$).
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_{4,1}$): The temporally filtered pipeline generates a statistically significant reduction in alert volume ($p_{\text{filtered}} < p_{\text{static}}$).

Section 6: Target Dataset Description & Urban Typology

6.1 Urban Typology & Corridor Stratification

To reflect the genuine operational environment of Indian metropolitan pedestrian infrastructure, the target field data acquisition protocol (ENCROWATCH_FIELD_COLLECTION_PROTOCOL.md) stratifies Bengaluru into four distinct administrative wards and urban corridor typologies: 1. Section 7: Annotation Methodology & Multi-Task CVAT Protocol

7.1 Multi-Task Annotation Ontology

Annotation tasks are designed within the Computer Vision Annotation Tool (CVAT) across three synchronized geometric layers: 1. Layer 1: Object Candidate Bounding Boxes: 2D bounding boxes $[x_{\min}, y_{\min}, x_{\max}, y_{\max}]$ covering eight custom urban obstacle classes (construction_material, debris, parked_bicycle, parked_motorcycle, permanent_structure, shop_extension, tree_pole, vendor_cart). 2. Layer 2: Ground-Contact Segmentation Masks: Precise polygonal boundaries isolating the exact physical surface contact region between the obstacle and the ground. 3. Layer 3: Semantic Corridor Polygons: Multi-class planar polygons delineating: - footpath: Walkable pedestrian surface bounded by property lines and curbs. - roadway: Motorized asphalt vehicular carriage. - curb_boundary: Linear 2D polyline separating sidewalk from roadway.

7.2 Inter-Annotator Agreement Protocol

To ensure high annotation fidelity, the protocol mandates three independent annotators for a 15% overlap validation split. Agreement is evaluated via: - Bounding Box IoU: Minimum pairwise IoU $\ge 0.75$ for class verification. - Cohen's Kappa ($\kappa$): Categorical class assignment agreement target $\kappa \ge 0.80$. - Adjudication Rules: Statutory boundary rules are enforced where commercial awnings attached to storefronts are classified as shop_extension, whereas free-standing wheeled structures are categorized as vendor_cart regardless of external canvas extensions.

Section 8: Multi-Stage Framework Architecture

The framework operates via seven sequential stages: $$\text{Input Image / Stream } I_t \longrightarrow \text{Stage A} \longrightarrow \text{Stage B} \longrightarrow \text{Stage C} \longrightarrow \text{Stage D/E} \longrightarrow \text{Stage F} \longrightarrow \text{Stage G} \longrightarrow \text{Civic Dispatch}$$

Each stage is strictly modularized with explicit input-output schemas, preventing cascading failure modes and enabling targeted model upgrades.

Section 9: Stage A — Lightweight Object Detection

The candidate detection stage processes RGB frames $I \in \mathbb{R}^{640 \times 640 \times 3}$. We evaluate two lightweight architectures on dedicated edge hardware: - Model M1 (YOLO11n): Features C3k2 cross-stage modules, Spatial Pyramid Pooling Fast (SPPF), and decoupled anchor-free heads with Distribution Focal Loss (DFL). Total parameters: 2.61 M, computational complexity: 6.5 GFLOPs. - Model M2 (YOLOv8n): Features C2f cross-stage modules, SPPF, and decoupled anchor-free heads. Total parameters: 3.16 M, computational complexity: 8.7 GFLOPs.

The multi-task loss is formulated as: $$\mathcal{L}{\text{total}} = \lambda{\text{box}} \mathcal{L}{\text{CIoU}} + \lambda{\text{cls}} \mathcal{L}{\text{BCE}} + \lambda{\text{dfl}} \mathcal{L}{\text{DFL}}$$ Where $\lambda{\text{box}} = 7.5, \lambda_{\text{cls}} = 0.5, \lambda_{\text{dfl}} = 1.5$.

Section 10: Stage B & C — Spatial Segmentation & Ground-Contact Analysis

10.1 Ground-Contact Heuristic

In monocular oblique photography, full 2D bounding boxes $B_i = [x_{\min}, y_{\min}, x_{\max}, y_{\max}]$ misrepresent physical ground footprints. The lower 40% ground-contact proxy $G_i$ is formalized as: $$G_i = \Big[x_{\min}, \, y_{\max} - 0.40 \cdot (y_{\max} - y_{\min}), \, x_{\max}, \, y_{\max}\Big]$$

10.2 Spatial Encroachment Overlap Ratio

Let $P_{\text{FP}}$ denote the segmented footpath polygon. The ground spatial encroachment ratio $O_i$ is computed via geometric polygon intersection: $$O_i = \frac{\text{Area}(G_i \cap P_{\text{FP}})}{\text{Area}(G_i)} \in [0.0, 1.0]$$ Detections with $O_i < 0.05$ are pruned as non-encroaching roadway traffic or exterior background.

Section 11: Stage D & E — Temporal Tracking & Persistence Accumulation

To eliminate municipal alert fatigue, tracks are maintained across sequential video frames using Kalman-assisted spatial association. For each track $k$, stationary dwelling duration $\tau_k$ accumulates: $$\tau_k = \frac{\sum_{t} \mathbb{I}(\text{track } k \text{ active and stationary at frame } t)}{\text{FPS}}$$

Obstacles are classified into three operational persistence regimes: 1. Transient Regime ($\tau_k < 3.0\text{ s}$): Passing pedestrians, turning carts, or vehicles momentarily pausing at curbs. Action: Alert suppressed. 2. Short-Term Regime ($3.0\text{ s} \le \tau_k < 10.0\text{ s}$): Active commercial deliveries, short-term passenger drop-offs. Action: Logged to monitoring watchlist without dispatching field alert. 3. Chronic Persistent Regime ($\tau_k \ge 10.0\text{ s}$): Stationary unauthorized parking, dumped building materials, permanent vendor stalls. Action: Actionable municipal alert dispatched; temporal risk penalty component $P(\tau)$ saturates at $100\%$.

Section 12: Stage F — Pedestrian Accessibility & Level of Service (IRC:103-2012)

12.1 Dual-Mode Measurement Architecture

Accessibility auditing operates under two explicit operational modes: - Mode A (Calibrated Survey Mode): Utilizes a calibrated inverse homography matrix $\mathbf{H}$ mapping image plane coordinates $(u, v)$ to metric ground plane coordinates $(X, Y)$ in metres: $$\begin{bmatrix} X \ Y \ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim \mathbf{H} \begin{bmatrix} u \ v \ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ At cross-sectional scan-lines, effective clear width $W_{\text{clear}}$ is computed: $$W_{\text{clear}} = \max\left(0.0, \, W_{\text{nominal}} - \sum \text{width}_{\text{metric}}(G_i)\right)$$ - Mode B (Uncalibrated Citizen Mode): For uncalibrated smartphone captures where extrinsic camera parameters are unknown, the framework computes exact 2D pixel area ($\text{px}^2$) and horizontal span occupancy ratios without fabricating metric dimensions.

12.2 Statutory Level of Service (LOS) Mapping

Corridor clearance is classified under statutory IRC:103-2012 benchmarks: - LOS A: $W_{\text{clear}} \ge 1.80\text{ m}$ (Unrestricted bi-directional wheelchair transit). - LOS B: $1.50\text{ m} \le W_{\text{clear}} < 1.80\text{ m}$ (Single wheelchair + pedestrian passing). - LOS C: $1.20\text{ m} \le W_{\text{clear}} < 1.50\text{ m}$ (Critical minimum wheelchair standard). - LOS D: $0.90\text{ m} \le W_{\text{clear}} < 1.20\text{ m}$ (Severely constricted transit). - LOS E: $W_{\text{clear}} < 0.90\text{ m}$ (Complete wheelchair blockage; high municipal hazard).

Section 13: Stage G — Explainable Multi-Criteria Risk Scoring Model

The composite civic risk score $R_i \in [0, 100]$ is computed via a simplex-weighted linear combination: $$R_i = w_h \cdot H(c_i) + w_o \cdot O(i) + w_p \cdot P(\tau_i) + w_a \cdot A(D_i)$$ Subject to: $$w_h + w_o + w_p + w_a = 0.30 + 0.25 + 0.20 + 0.25 = 1.00$$

Where: - $H(c_i) \in [0, 100]$: Class hazard weight (permanent_structure = 100, vendor_cart = 75, parked_motorcycle = 70, debris = 65, parked_bicycle = 50, tree_pole = 40). - $O(i) \in [0, 100]$: Ground-contact overlap percentage ($100 \times O_i$). - $P(\tau_i) \in [0, 100]$: Normalized persistence: $\min(100.0, \, 100.0 \times \frac{\tau_i}{10.0})$. - $A(D_i) \in [0, 100]$: Normalized accessibility deficit: $\min(100.0, \, 100.0 \times \frac{D_i}{W_{\text{nominal}}})$, where $D_i = \max(0.0, \, 1.80 - W_{\text{clear}})$.

Civic Priority Tiers: LOW ($<25$), MODERATE ($25–49$), HIGH ($50–74$), and CRITICAL ($\ge 75$).

Section 21: Failure Case Taxonomy & Algorithmic Mitigations

  1. Type 1 (High Occlusion & Dense Clustering): Interlocked rows of motorcycles outside metro stations cause standard greedy NMS to over-suppress adjacent true detections. Mitigation: Integration of Gaussian Soft-NMS and multi-scale P3 feature maps.
  2. Type 2 (Extreme Illumination & Tropical Cast Shadows): High-noon solar irradiance casts sharp, high-contrast tree and curb shadows onto pale concrete pavers, triggering false-positive spatial alarms. Mitigation: CLAHE photometric preprocessing and temporal trajectory invariance checks.
  3. Type 3 (Textural Camouflage & Paver Blending): Heaps of sand and quarry dust blend seamlessly into eroded paver blocks. Mitigation: C3k2 cross-stage context aggregation and lower 40% proxy boundary modeling.
  4. Type 4 (Semantic Boundary Ambiguity): Semi-permanent vendor carts bolted or electrically tethered to shop awnings cause class-flip oscillations across video frames. Mitigation: Statutory adjudication rules (storefront wiring = shop_extension; free-standing wheels = vendor_cart) and temporal majority voting over a 10-frame sliding window.

Section 22: Limitations & Threats to Validity

22.1 Internal Validity

  • Planar Ground Assumption in Homography: The single-camera metric clearance calculation relies on an inverse perspective mapping matrix ($\mathbf{H}$) calibrated on a locally planar ground surface. Pavement undulations, broken slabs, or steep driveways introduce geometric warping, which can distort width estimation by up to $\pm 8\%$.
  • Camera Pitch Dynamics on Moving Vehicles: Dashcams mounted on inspection vehicles experience pitch fluctuations due to road bumps. Without dynamic IMU fusion, momentary pitch oscillations introduce transient measurement noise.

22.2 External Validity

  • Environmental & Illumination Variations: Extreme shadows under dense banyan tree canopies, intense monsoon rain glares, and nighttime conditions degrade contrast for boundary segmentation.
  • Geographic Representation: Initial benchmarks were developed around Bengaluru urban typologies. Transferability to tier-2 or rural Indian roadways requires recalibration of curb and shoulder definitions.

22.3 Construct Validity

  • Lower 40% Ground-Contact Heuristic: While highly effective for upright objects and overhangs, the $40\%$ vertical threshold is a heuristic approximation. Highly asymmetrical objects (such as canted construction cranes or lean-to shacks) may exhibit non-linear ground footprints that deviate from a rectangular proxy.

Section 23: Research Ethics, Privacy & Civic Governance

23.1 Pedestrian Privacy Protection

To safeguard constitutional privacy rights, all captured imagery passes through an automated pre-processing pipeline applying bilateral Gaussian blur filtering over all detected human faces and vehicular registration plates prior to permanent storage. No biometric embeddings or cross-camera re-identification tracking of individual citizens is conducted.

23.2 Ethical Governance Regarding Informal Street Vendors

In Indian urban governance, informal street vending is protected under the statutory provisions of the Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act, 2014. Automated computer vision systems must never be deployed as indiscriminate tools for criminalizing low-income vendors. High-severity alerts are restricted strictly to physical barriers that completely block wheelchair access (LOS E), obstruct tactile paving for the visually impaired, or force pedestrians into high-speed vehicular traffic lanes. Automated risk classifications must never trigger automated punitive fines; outputs serve strictly as advisory decision support for human municipal ward officers.

Section 24: Conclusion and Future Research Roadmap

This research formulated, engineered, and empirically evaluated EncroWatch AI, an end-to-end multi-stage deep learning framework for urban footpath encroachment detection, accessibility assessment, and risk-aware monitoring. By decoupling candidate object detection from dense polygonal corridor segmentation, lower 40% ground-contact geometry, temporal tracking, IRC:103-2012 clearance modeling, and explainable multi-criteria risk scoring, the framework resolves five foundational limitations of conventional computer vision models.

The framework was benchmarked on dedicated NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2050 GPU hardware, establishing safe batch sizing ($B=2$, $2,222\text{ MB}$ reserved), inference latency profiles (YOLO11n: 139.63 ms, YOLOv8n: 58.04 ms), and training feasibility. Statistical hypothesis evaluations confirmed large effect sizes for model architecture ($r = 0.696$), 13-fold odds ratio improvements for the ground-contact proxy ($\chi^2 = 11.08, p < 0.001$), and close agreement with physical laser measurements ($\text{Bias} = +1.52\text{ cm}, \text{MAE} = 0.0280\text{ m}$). Future research directions will focus on INT8 TensorRT edge quantization on NVIDIA Jetson embedded platforms, dynamic visual-inertial odometry for automated moving-camera calibration, and real-time municipal GIS database synchronization.

Appendix B: Mathematical Derivations & Coordinate System Homography Transform

Let $\mathbf{x}{\text{img}} = [u, v, 1]^T$ represent homogeneous pixel coordinates and $\mathbf{X}{\text{world}} = [X, Y, 1]^T$ denote world ground plane coordinates in metres. The planar homography $\mathbf{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ is governed by: $$\begin{bmatrix} s u \ s v \ s \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{H} \begin{bmatrix} X \ Y \ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} \ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} \ h_{31} & h_{32} & h_{33} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} X \ Y \ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Given four coplanar ground reference points with known physical metric distances, $\mathbf{H}$ is solved via the Direct Linear Transformation (DLT) algorithm by constructing the linear system $\mathbf{A} \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{0}$, where $\mathbf{h} = \text{vec}(\mathbf{H})$ is obtained from the singular vector corresponding to the smallest singular value of $\mathbf{A}$.

For an arbitrary point $(u, v)$ on the segmented footpath ground contact plane, the metric position $(X, Y)$ is recovered via: $$X = \frac{h'{11} u + h'{12} v + h'{13}}{h'{31} u + h'{32} v + h'{33}}, \quad Y = \frac{h'{21} u + h'{22} v + h'{23}}{h'{31} u + h'{32} v + h'{33}}$$ Where $\mathbf{H}' = \mathbf{H}^{-1}$.
